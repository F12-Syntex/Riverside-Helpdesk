1. Duty Doctor Week – Urgent Red Slots

For the week commencing 7 September 2026, please ensure urgent or potentially serious concerns are brought to the Duty Doctor’s attention promptly and booked into the Urgent Red slots.

This includes:

  • Patients reporting potentially serious symptoms, including chest pain, breathing difficulties, bleeding in pregnancy or suicidal thoughts/concerns or any safe guarding concerns.
  • Urgent calls from Social Services or other healthcare professionals, including District Nurses, hospital doctors/teams and Coroners.
  • Housebound patients requesting an appointment, particularly elderly patients, those with multiple health conditions and those receiving palliative care.
  • Unwell newborns and very young babies.

Elderly/frail patients and young babies can deteriorate quickly. Do not leave urgent concerns in a routine task queue. 

Messaging and adding patients to urgent slots

If you are unsure whether a concern requires urgent review:

  • Send an urgent message toDr Rishi, or use the routine screen-messaging route to Dr Goel for advice.
  • Ensure the message is picked up. If there is no prompt response and the concern cannot safely wait, escalate directly.
  • Once the doctor confirms urgent review is required, add the patient to the Urgent Red slots.
  • Immediately tell the doctor that the patient has been added, so they know the patient is awaiting their call.

Before sending a task asking a GP to review or act on a result, check who originally requested the test.

The GP who signed off or acknowledged a result is not necessarily the original requester or the clinician responsible for ongoing follow-up.

Please direct queries as follows:

  • GP-requested tests: send to the GP who originally requested the test.
  • Tests requested by Iqra, Mishalu or any pharmacist: send to Dr Goel.

If the original clinician is away and the matter cannot safely wait, escalate to duty doctor to review.

5. Check Consultations, Maintain Continuity and Avoid Duplication

Please read the relevant consultations before taking action on a patient query.

For an ongoing matter, direct the query to the same GP already managing the issue, wherever possible. If that GP is away, check the recorded plan and cover arrangements. Do not leave a time-sensitive matter waiting for their return.

Before sending another task, booking an appointment or requesting a clinician’s call, check whether:

  • The patient has already been contacted.
  • The issue has already been dealt with.
  • A task or appointment already exists.
  • There is a documented plan.

Patients who are now acutely unwell require prompt escalation, regardless of who previously managed their care.

MCS swabs

Swabs for microscopy, culture and sensitivity (MCS) are kept in the downstairs storeroom. The Practice Manager has also left a small supply at reception - These are purple top tubes. 

Please check the requested test and provide the correct swab usually mentioned on the request test paper. .

Two stool tests on one request form

Reception raised concerns about two different stool tests appear on the same form.

Where separate forms are required:

  1. Print two copies of the request form.
  2. On each copy, clearly cross out the test that does not relate to that sample and remove the corresponding test sticker.
  3. Ensure each form and container clearly relates to one different test.
  4. Explain to the patient that these are two different tests, so they do not mix up the tests to ensure they do no do the same stool test twice.

Do not alter patient-identification details and check any uncertainty before giving the forms or containers to the patient.

Nail clippings, sputum and semen samples

We discussed the white-top containers used for nail clippings and certain other samples, including sputum. Always check the specific test requirements.

For semen analysis requested by a fertility clinic, patient must follow the clinic’s instructions for the collection arrangements and delivery times. These samples are not brought back to the practice.

8. B12 Injections – Starting Treatment and Follow-Up

The doctor identifies patients who require B12 injections, informs the patient and confirms the treatment schedule. Reception must follow the documented clinical plan.

For patients prescribed the practice’s usual six-dose loading course:

  • Book six injections over two weeks.
  • Arrange three appointments in the first week and three in the second week.
  • The preferred pattern is Monday, Wednesday and Friday, subject to availability and the prescribed dosing intervals.
  • If this pattern is unavailable, check suitable alternative dates with the nurse.
  • Following the loading course, book maintenance injections every three months where this is the prescribed plan.

Do not assume every patient follows the same B12 schedule. Check with the nurse or prescribing doctor if the instructions differ or are unclear.

B12 vials are prescribed for the patient to collect from their pharmacy. Remind patients to:

  • Collect their prescribed vials.
  • Bring a vial to each injection appointment.
  • Request further supplies before they run out or expire.
  • Collect the next supply before further routine injection appointments.

9. NHS Pharmacy First – Conditions, Age Limits and Treatment

Participating community pharmacies in England can assess and treat seven conditions through NHS Pharmacy First, including supplying prescription-only medicines where appropriate, without a GP prescription.

Antibiotics are not guaranteed. The pharmacist must assess the patient and check symptoms, treatment criteria, medical history, allergies and medicines.

Conditions and age limits

Condition

Eligible age

Treatment and key points

Sore throat

5 years and over

Antibiotics may be supplied for a suspected bacterial infection where the pharmacist’s assessment supports this. Many sore throats only require self-care.

Sinusitis

12 years and over

Treatment may include a steroid nasal spray or antibiotics where appropriate. Prescription treatment generally requires symptoms lasting 10 days or more with little improvement.

Earache – acute middle-ear infection

1–17 years inclusive

The pharmacist needs to examine the ear. Antibiotics may be supplied if indicated; some patients receive pain-relief advice or treatment instead.

Infected insect bites or stings

1 year and over

Antibiotics may be supplied for bacterial infection. An itchy or swollen bite alone does not mean antibiotics are needed.

Impetigo

1 year and over

Treatment for non-bullous impetigo may include antiseptic cream, antibiotic cream or oral antibiotics, depending on the extent and assessment.

Uncomplicated lower urinary tract infection (UTI)

Women aged 16–64 inclusive

Antibiotics may be supplied where the patient meets the uncomplicated UTI criteria. Important exclusions are listed below.

Shingles

18 years and over

Eligible patients may receive antiviral medication, rather than antibiotics. Prompt assessment is important.

Age ranges are confirmed by the NHS Pharmacy First information. Treatment follows the NHS England clinical pathways and medicine protocols.

Important conditions and exclusions

UTIs

The Pharmacy First treatment pathway excludes:

  • Men.
  • Patients outside the age range.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding patients.
  • Patients with urinary catheters.
  • Recurrent UTIs: 2 episodes in 6 months or 3 in 12 months.

These patients need an appropriate clinical review. Fever, shivering, pain in the back/side under the ribs, vomiting or marked illness require urgent assessment for a possible kidney infection. Other medical and medicine-related exclusions also apply.

Infected bites or stings

The antibiotic protocol requires clear evidence of infection present or worsening at least 48 hours after the bite/sting, with additional clinical signs assessed by the pharmacist.

Do not tell a rapidly deteriorating patient to wait 48 hours.

Impetigo

Both localised and widespread non-bullous impetigo can be treated within the service when appropriate. Bullous impetigo—large, fluid-filled blisters—requires medical assessment.

Shingles

Refer promptly, ideally within 72 hours of the rash appearing. Some patients may still qualify for treatment up to 7 days after onset.

Pregnancy, immunosuppression or symptoms affecting the eye require medical assessment rather than routine pharmacy redirection.

Reception responsibilities

Check the patient’s age, main symptoms, duration and urgent concerns, then follow the agreed referral process.

The pharmacist makes the final clinical and treatment decision. This summary is not a complete exclusion checklist.

Patients can approach a participating pharmacy directly for these seven conditions or be referred by the practice. Patients outside these treatment pathways may still obtain pharmacy advice .

Suggested wording:

“The pharmacist can assess this condition through Pharmacy First and provide treatment, including antibiotics if clinically appropriate. They will check whether the service and treatment are suitable for you.”

10. Prescription Requests – Messages and Collection

We discussed patient feedback about not receiving a message whenever a prescription has been issued.

The practice receives over 100 prescription requests a day, so it is not feasible to send an individual confirmation message for every prescription signed.

Our routine approach is to contact patients when their request has been rejected or will not be issued, explaining the reason and next steps.

Please explain that there are two separate stages:

  1. The prescriber authorises and issues the prescription.
  2. The pharmacy dispenses the medication and prepares it for collection.

A prescription being issued does not mean the medication is immediately ready to collect.

Patients who would like a notification when their medication is ready should ask their nominated pharmacy whether it offers a text-message or other collection notification service.

Suggested wording:

“We do not routinely send a message when a prescription has been issued. Once it has been authorised, your pharmacy still needs to dispense the medication and prepare it for collection. Please speak to your pharmacy about receiving a notification when it is ready. We will contact you if your request cannot be issued or further information is needed.”

11. Patient Confidentiality at Reception

Please remember that conversations at reception can be overheard.

When discussing a patient with colleagues, use their EMIS number or initials where appropriate, rather than repeatedly saying their full name aloud.

Avoid discussing confidential clinical details in the public reception area. Continue to carry out the required identity checks when dealing directly with a patient where possible advise them to write their details down on a paper then saying it out loud..

12. Do Not Routinely Tell Patients to “Call Back”

Avoid telling patients to call back another day when we can record or submit their request on their behalf.

Explain the next step and expected response arrangements. Where contact on the requested day has been agreed, advise the patient accordingly.

Exceptions may include requests for appointments several weeks ahead when the relevant rota is not yet available. Explain the situation and provide the appropriate next step, avoiding unnecessary barriers to access.

16. End-of-Day and Locking-Up Responsibilities

Everyone responsible for closing must ensure the practice is left safe, secure and confidential.
